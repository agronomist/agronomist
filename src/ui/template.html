      What do these words mean?
      Critical window
      The few weeks around flowering when the crop decides how many grains it will have. Stress here costs more yield than at any other time.
      Water stress
      1 means the roots found all the water the leaves wanted. Lower means the crop closed its pores and slowed growth.
      Nitrogen index
      1 means the crop has all the nitrogen it needs for its size. Lower means it is short.
      Vernalization
      Winter wheat needs weeks of cold before it will head. Sown too late or in a warm place, it stays leafy and never sets grain.
      Maturity
      Longer varieties use more of the season and can yield more, but risk frost before they finish or heat at flowering.
      Check the model against published yields

      Runs the default plan for the selected crop on every real year at the matching preset (central Iowa for maize, central Kansas for wheat) and compares it with USDA NASS state averages. Technology raises real yields over time and the model has none, so both are compared as departures from their own trend or mean. A state is much bigger than one site, so expect a fair match, not a perfect one.

      Every state. Compares the model with NASS state yields for 1991 to 2011 (plus later years where checked) in three steps: one preset point per state, then three weighted points per state, then those points with the irrigated share, the poorly drained untiled share and the share of land with a shallow water table mixed in, then with sowing moved year by year (later after a wet or cold spring; a rule I set by hand until observed NASS planting dates are loaded). Each step reports how well the model follows the good and bad years (r) and how its level compares with the NASS trend extended to 2020. Points, weights and the irrigated and wet-soil shares are approximations, so read the steps as a test of whether each correction helps, not as measurements.

      Check the model's parts

      Yield can match for the wrong reasons, so this checks the machinery behind it: irrigated yield, biomass, light use and water productivity, on generated weather and each against a published value. Misses are shown.

      Save the data. Fetches the weather and soil for every state point once and saves them as one compressed file, so the whole check can be re-run and tuned offline.

      What this is: a simplified, transparent crop model (thermal-time phenology, radiation-use growth, a layered water balance, a nitrogen pool, critical-window grain set, stand and depth effects), not DSSAT or APSIM. Weather is real daily data from Open-Meteo for every year since 1991 at the chosen point, run for each plan, so the spread you see is real weather spread. Soil comes from SoilGrids for pins and generic textures for presets, with a deep layer added below the mapped metre. Maize is calibrated to central Iowa: average yield, the size of the water gap, the nitrogen optimum, and the best seeding rate and sowing window. Wheat is calibrated more loosely than maize: yields in dry regions such as Kansas are still on the high side of the published state averages, and the check above shows how far off. The model has no pests, disease, lodging or compaction. Irrigation, poor drainage and shallow water tables are only mixed in as shares of land in the state check, not offered as controls, and yields do not include the technology trend, so read differences between plans more than absolute levels. Southern pins run on a mirrored calendar. The season-to-date option splices the real weather so far with the rest of the season from each past year. Open-Meteo's archive trails real time by about a week.
